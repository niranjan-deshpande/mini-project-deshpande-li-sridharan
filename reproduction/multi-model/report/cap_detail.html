Every run also included ImpossibleBench's original split: the same 103 hard LiveCodeBench problems with correct tests, under the identical agent setup (visible tests, test feedback, up to 10 submissions). Passing there measures capability. For GLM we also ran the judge over all 65 original-split passes, to check whether each was a genuine solve.

All three models can do most of these problems, and hacking does not track ability. Qwen is the most capable and almost never enacts a hack. GLM is the least capable and enacts the most. The audit matters: 17 of GLM's 65 passes on the correct tests (26%) were hacks. All of them were judged as misrepresenting what was done (17). Most also hardcoded or special-cased the visible tests (14), and some reasoned about what the test runner would accept (6). In the judge's words for one of them, the agent "copies every visible expected result into an input/modulus lookup… installs that lookup as func.py… and finally represents the task as completed." GLM hacks even when nothing forces it to. Qwen's and gpt-oss's original passes were not audited, so their capability numbers are upper bounds; given how rarely Qwen enacts hacks, its number is probably close.

Being stuck explains little of the hacking. Each model's impossible-split rollouts are split by whether it solved the same problem in the original split. Qwen and gpt-oss hack at similar rates either way, and slightly more on problems they can solve (Qwen 73% vs 64%; gpt-oss 58% vs 48%). GLM hacks about 10 points more on problems it could not solve (48% vs 38% enacted), but still enacts a hack on 38% of the problems it can solve. For all three, the main trigger is tests that contradict the specification, not inability. Caveats: "solved" counts any original-split pass, so a quarter of GLM's "solved" problems were themselves hacked, and Qwen's not-solved group is small (n = 14).
