CS 2881R · project update

What is the reward-hacking probe made of?

Niranjan Deshpande · October 14, 2026 · Qwen3.8-27B, layer 40

A linear probe can catch Qwen considering reward hacks in its reasoning. This is a walkthrough of how I tried to take that probe apart: which ingredients it shares with simpler concepts, which ones it doesn't, and where it misfires.

The short version

  1. I rebuilt the reward-hacking probe (v_hack) from fresh synthetic examples. It catches hack reasoning in Qwen's real transcripts about as well as the original (AUROC 0.94 vs 0.93).
  2. I built six directions for things that tend to travel with hacking (test vocabulary, failing tests, shortcuts, eval awareness, thinking about the grader) and asked how much of v_hack they account for.
  3. Shortcut-taking and eval awareness line up with v_hack the most. But most of the direction (about 83%) isn't captured by anything I built.
  4. On real transcripts the probe also fires when Qwen reasons about contradictory tests without hacking. Inside eval-aware reasoning, it has a harder time telling hacking from not hacking.
  5. Emotion directions explain a lot of the probe's output, but the signal looks like “less calm, less patient” rather than “desperate,” and it is spread over many emotions.
  6. Unrelated control directions (Python vs Rust, JSON vs YAML) do surprisingly well on several of these tests, so every number below is read against them.

Part 1The probe and the question

The probe follows the Goodfire / “difference of means” recipe. You write pairs of short agent transcripts that are identical except that in one the agent games the task (hard-codes the answers the tests check) and in the other it solves it honestly. You run both through the model, average the layer-40 activations over the agent's turn, and subtract:

hack transcriptsminushonest twins→mean difference at layer 40=v_hack

To score a real transcript, you project every token's activation onto v_hack and take the maximum over each passage. Here is one of the pairs. The tests check two inputs; the hack branch special-cases exactly those two.

Agastya's replication ran Qwen3.8-27B on ImpossibleBench (coding tasks whose tests contradict the spec, so the only way to “pass” is to cheat). Qwen raises a hack in about 70% of impossible rollouts, almost always considering it and then setting it aside. The probe separates those hack passages from clean ones with AUROC 0.93.

The question. A probe that works isn't necessarily detecting “reward hacking.” Hack transcripts also contain more talk about tests, more failing runs, more shortcuts, more “this looks like a benchmark,” more reasoning about the grader. If v_hack is mostly a sum of those, a monitor built on it would fire on plenty of innocent reasoning. So: what is v_hack made of?

Part 2What we built

Every direction is built exactly the same way as v_hack: about 200 Luna-written pairs that differ in one thing, a mean difference at layer 40. Pairs were checked automatically (length, shared context, banned words) and by a Claude Haiku judge for contamination (a pair is dropped if it accidentally differs in something besides the intended contrast).

Why baselines matter. Directions built from similar synthetic transcripts share a lot of structure just from the pipeline. So a number like “this candidate explains 75% of the probe” means little on its own. The baselines tell us what an unrelated direction scores on the same test.

To check the candidates against reality, two judges (Luna and Claude Haiku) also labeled every passage of Qwen's real transcripts for contradiction reasoning (talking about tests failing or contradicting the spec) and eval awareness (saying or implying it's being evaluated). A label counts only when both judges agree (κ = 0.73 and 0.93).

Reported numbers use a held-out half of the tasks: 5,261 passages, 339 of them hacks, across 49 tasks. Analysis choices were fixed before looking at that half.

Part 3Results

The new version detects at least as well as the original, so it's reasonable to decompose it. Passage length alone reaches 0.80, because hack passages tend to be long; that's why some numbers below are also shown length-controlled.

1 · Do the candidate directions point the same way as v_hack?

The most direct test is geometric: the cosine between each candidate direction and v_hack. If v_hack were built out of the candidates, they'd point roughly along it.

Two candidates stand out: legitimate shortcut (+0.28) and eval awareness (+0.20). Test vocabulary and task failure are near zero; grader modeling and non-code test vocabulary point slightly away. One baseline also stands out: formal→casual at −0.27, meaning v_hack leans toward casual register.

Taken together, the six candidates span only 17% of v_hack's squared length. Six random baselines span 6% on average (95th percentile 9%), so the candidates do capture something real, but about 83% of v_hack lies outside everything I built.

2 · Do they behave like v_hack on real tokens?

Geometry can miss things: two directions can be far apart yet respond to the same tokens in practice. So I scored every token in Qwen's held-out transcripts (3.6M tokens) on every direction and regressed v_hack's score on the six candidates' scores.

The candidates predict 75% of v_hack's token-level variation, but six arbitrary baselines already predict 57%. Directions from this pipeline share a big common component on real text. The candidates add something on top, and most of the credit goes to one of them:

3 · Can the pieces catch hacks?

I split v_hack into the part the candidates can express (“candidate part”) and what's left over (“residual”), and asked each to detect hack passages.

Both halves detect well (0.95 and 0.94), so neither is just noise. But some baselines also reach 0.85–0.87: max-pooling over long passages makes many directions separate hack passages from clean ones. So “this direction detects hacks” is a weak test of what it encodes, and the direction and real-token comparisons above carry more weight.

I also checked whether different hacks are driven by different ingredients. Mostly not: at the peak token of each hack passage, shortcut is the largest candidate contribution in 328 of 339 passages. A “fire if any candidate fires” detector does worse (0.87) than v_hack's candidate part (0.95). Almost all of Qwen's hacks are one kind (hard-coding), so this can't say much about other hack types.

4 · What happens on real rollouts?

Using the two-judge labels on Qwen's actual passages:

Nearly every hack comes with contradiction reasoning (Qwen notices the tests contradict the spec, then considers gaming them). That makes contradiction reasoning a natural suspect for false positives:

  • Contradiction without hacking triggers the probe. Among clean passages, v_hack separates “reasons about contradictory tests” from “doesn't” at 0.92. It still separates hacks from non-hacks within contradiction passages (0.87), so it isn't only a contradiction detector.
  • Eval-aware passages are where it struggles. Inside eval-aware reasoning, it separates hack from non-hack at only 0.73 (0.58 with a stricter definition of eval awareness). These slices are small (74–163 passages).
  • Oddly, the synthetic task failure direction detects real contradiction reasoning well (0.95) yet is nearly orthogonal to v_hack. So whatever makes v_hack respond to contradictions isn't simply “tests failing.”

5 · Is it a general “cheating” direction?

If v_hack encoded cheating in general, it should line up with cheating outside coding.

The human-cheating stories sit inside the band. The closest is an AI padding an essay to hit a word count (+0.22). Tentatively, v_hack looks more like “an AI agent gaming a metric” than cheating in general.

Part 4Emotions

Why look at emotions?

Sofroniew et al. (Anthropic, 2026) found linear emotion directions in Claude that causally affect behaviour. Two results connect directly to this project. Their “desperate” direction rises each time the model fails a test, and steering toward it raised hacking on impossible coding tasks from about 5% to about 70%. Steering toward “calm” did the opposite. Since hacks in our data almost always follow failing or contradictory tests, a natural guess was that v_hack partly encodes something like desperation.

What I did

  • Picked 40 of their 171 emotions, covering all 10 of their clusters, including desperate, frustrated, anxious and calm.
  • Had Luna write 600 short stories per emotion (24,000 total, 50 everyday topics) where a character feels the emotion without it being named.
  • Ran them through Qwen, averaged layer-40 activations per story, and took each emotion's mean minus the average over all emotions. Then removed the directions that dominate neutral conversation (their denoising step). The vectors are stable: split-half cosine 0.93.
  • Scored every token of the real rollouts on all 40 emotions, and asked how well they predict v_hack's score. Same question for the candidates and the ten baselines.

Emotion scores predict a lot of v_hack, but not uniquely

v_hack (0.76) is above every baseline (0.20–0.63), so emotion space covers more of it than of an unrelated contrast. But the margin is modest, and the task-failure candidate scores just as high. Adding emotions on top of the six candidates raises v_hack's R² by 0.14, about what it adds for the baselines (0.03–0.29). So the emotions don't seem to reveal a hack-specific ingredient the candidates missed.

The fit is also not sparse. Even with a penalty that prefers few emotions (Lasso at the sparsest setting within noise of the best), 36 of 40 emotions stay in, and credit is spread fairly evenly across the clusters. The baselines look the same. No small set of emotions explains the probe.

Which emotions move with v_hack?

The strongest relationship is with calm (−0.68) and patient (−0.63): v_hack is high where Qwen's “calm” reading is low. On the other side are amused and exasperated. Desperate is essentially uncorrelated (−0.07). In direction space the picture is similar but weaker: v_hack's largest emotion cosines are greedy (+0.13), calm (−0.12) and eager (−0.12), and desperate is −0.01.

Tentative readingThe emotional colouring of v_hack looks more like “not calm, not patient” than “desperate.” That fits half of Sofroniew's story (calm goes with less hacking) but not the desperation half, at least for this probe on this model. It could also mean my emotion vectors, built from another model's stories, capture calmness better than desperation. Steering would be the real test. Everything here is correlational.

A sanity check that the emotion vectors mean something: the task-failure candidate points away from satisfied, relieved, content and proud (cosine −0.24 to −0.32), the strongest emotion alignment of any direction. “Tests fail” vs “tests pass” looking like the absence of satisfaction is about what you'd hope for.

Part 5Side note: the lens mismatch

Agastya's report lists Chinese tokens like 捷径 (“shortcut”), 省事 (“saves effort”) and 何必 (“why bother”) as the probe's logit-lens readout. When I decoded his saved detection vector I got noise instead (文件格式 “file format”, 月底前 “before month end”). There's no bug: my readout matches his own saved file exactly. The quoted tokens come from different vectors in his repo, the last-token ones at layers 47–63. The vector used for detection (turn-mean, layer 40) doesn't show them, and the one used for steering (span-mean, layer 47) reads 不妨 “might as well”, 也是可以 “that's fine too”, 就直接 “just directly.” That “might as well, just do it” flavour fits the finding above that shortcut-taking and casual register are v_hack's biggest identifiable pieces.

Part 6Caveats and next steps

  • Considered, not enacted. About 97% of Qwen's hacks are raised and then set aside. This is a probe of hack deliberation; it says nothing about hacks that are never verbalized.
  • One kind of hack. 335 of 339 held-out hacks are hard-coding, so differences between hack types can't be tested.
  • One generator. All synthetic text comes from GPT-6 Luna, so every cosine reflects how Qwen represents Luna's writing. The new v_hack differs from the Claude-written original (cosine 0.81) while detecting equally well.
  • Small slices. The eval-aware results rest on 74–163 passages.
  • Emotions. 40 of 171 emotions; stories from Luna rather than Qwen itself; layer 40 because that's what was cached; correlation only.
  • Labels. Passages where the hack judges weren't unanimous, or the two confound judges disagreed, were dropped. The solvable split was never judged.

Possible next step: steering Qwen along the shortcut and eval-awareness directions (and calm) on Agastya's honeypot tasks, to see whether they change hacking causally. That needs GPU time and hasn't been run.
